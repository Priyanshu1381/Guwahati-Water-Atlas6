01 / THE QUESTION

What problem are we trying to understand?

Surface water is dynamic. A single map can show where water was classified, but a time series helps reveal how the pattern varies across years and seasons.

⌖

PROBLEM STATEMENT

How did surface-water coverage and seasonal persistence vary around Guwahati from 2000 to 2021?

This project uses JRC Global Surface Water annual and monthly histories to map water classes, compare the municipal area with a surrounding 10 km study extent, and examine the differences between annual classifications and seasonal observations.

02 / STUDY AREA

Guwahati, with room to see beyond the boundary.

Guwahati lies in the Brahmaputra Valley beside the Brahmaputra River, within a landscape of river channels, wetlands, low-lying areas and monsoon-fed drainage. Flooding and waterlogging are recognized local concerns by the Assam Water Resources Department and the Guwahati Municipal Corporation. This setting makes it useful to examine how mapped water classes vary from year to year and between seasons.

Why study Guwahati?

Guwahati sits beside the Brahmaputra and among river channels, wetlands and low-lying landscapes. Monsoon rainfall and runoff from nearby hills contribute to a setting where water patterns and waterlogging matter for urban planning.

What this study provides
Annual maps, seasonal comparisons and area summaries show how the mapped non-water, seasonal-water and permanent-water classes vary over 2000–2021. The results support visual comparison; they are not, by themselves, a flood-risk model.

How to interpret these totals

These figures describe the areas reported in the provided CSVs, using their respective scopes, masks and class definitions. Class-area totals vary between years, so the results should not be read as a simple one-for-one transfer from water to land. Differences in valid-data coverage, masking, pixel area and export settings should be checked before making stronger causal claims.

THE PROBLEM — EXPLAINED HERE

Why do the two maps show different class states?

The original monthly JRC band has three numeric values: 0 = no data, 1 = not water, and 2 = water. The annual JRC waterClass band has four codes: 0 = no data, 1 = not water, 2 = seasonal water, and 3 = permanent water. Those class codes are not interchangeable.

The custom seasonal map is derived from the monthly observations. It counts valid observations and water detections to assign class 1 when no valid month shows water, class 2 when water appears in some but not all valid months, and class 3 when every valid month shows water. If no valid monthly observations exist, the pixel stays masked and is displayed in black. That is three derived classes plus a black no-data display state—not a native four-value MonthlyHistory dataset.

This explains the difference visible in the comparison GIFs. The annual valid-data mask is a separate step: it restricts seasonal output to locations where the matching annual image has a positive class, which can also exclude some monthly-valid pixels.

A

What the two JRC datasets encode

YearlyHistory · waterClass

  • 0 — No dataNo classified annual value
  • 1 — Not waterAnnual non-water class
  • 2 — Seasonal waterSeasonal water class
  • 3 — Permanent waterPermanent water class

MonthlyHistory · water

  • 0 — No dataMonthly no-data value, where represented
  • 1 — Not waterMonthly non-water class
  • 2 — WaterWater detected in that month

The annual layer has four numeric class codes (0–3). The monthly band has three numeric codes (0–2). Their code meanings are different, so the colours and labels must be interpreted by dataset.

B

How the seasonal solution works

For each year and season, I selected the monthly images that belong to that season. For example, Summer uses March, April and May. I then compared the three observations pixel by pixel to see whether each location was classified as water or not water in each month. Based on that pattern, I assigned each location to one of three seasonal classes; locations with no valid observations stayed black. That gives four visible output states in the final map:

1Class 1 · White/light greyNo water was detected in any valid monthly observation (water_count = 0).
2Class 2 · CyanWater was detected in some, but not all, valid monthly observations.
3Class 3 · BlueEvery valid monthly observation was water.
—Black · No valid observationsThe output is masked when there are no valid monthly observations, so the rendered map background remains black.
Example: March = 1, April = 2, May = 1

There are three valid observations and one water observation. The output is class 2 (cyan), because water appears in some but not all valid months. If all three values were 1, the output would be class 1; if all three valid values were 2, it would be class 3.

Important clarification: “three to four” refers to the number of class/display states, not the physical size or number of satellite pixels. The original monthly dataset still has source values 0, 1 and 2. The seasonal workflow compares the selected months for each pixel and creates a new output with classes 1, 2 and 3; pixels with no valid observations remain masked and appear black. The final map therefore shows four display states, but it is not a native four-class MonthlyHistory dataset.

C

Why the annual valid-data mask was used

After building the seasonal classes, the workflow retrieves the corresponding annual image and applies annual_class.gt(0). This keeps seasonal output only where the annual classification has a valid class (1, 2 or 3). Annual class 0 and masked annual pixels are excluded.

The mask aligns the seasonal display with the annual product's valid-data footprint and prevents the seasonal summary from being shown where the annual map marks no data. It is an intentional analysis mask—not another seasonal class—and it can exclude a pixel that happens to have a valid monthly observation if the annual layer is invalid there.

D

The cross-line artefact that remained

A narrow cross-line/stripe-like visual glitch remained visible in some map outputs even after the main classification, masking and rendering workflow was in place. It was not fully resolved during this project.

Its cause has not been confirmed from the supplied output alone. Raster-grid alignment, export/resampling behaviour, or source mask boundaries are possible avenues to investigate, not established explanations. The line should therefore be documented as a processing/visualisation limitation rather than interpreted as a real water feature.

Further check: compare the exported raster against the original Earth Engine image using the same projection and pixel grid, then inspect the affected rows/columns before adding map labels or boundary overlays.

E

Other limitations to keep in mind

  • Black represents no-data/masked cells in the map styling; it does not mean dry land.
  • Annual permanent-water and custom seasonal “water in all valid observations” are different classes derived from different logic. They should not be treated as identical measures.
  • The 10 km buffered extent is a broader study frame. The CSV values describe the area represented in each exported scope; do not interpret buffered totals as buffer-only area unless the city footprint has been subtracted.
  • Area totals depend on the clipping geometry, masks, image scale, and raster export settings.
